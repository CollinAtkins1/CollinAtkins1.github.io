## Project Overview

I led a team of four engineering students in the designing, building, and testing of an unpowered glider for an aerospace engineering competition.

Our goal was to build a lightweight glider that could maintain a stable, controlled flight while meeting the competition's size, weight, material, and budget requirements at the same time.

Throughout this project, I helped guide the team through the design process. The design process itself stretched from choosing materials and building the glider to conducting flight tests and evaluating its performance. We also completed simple engineering calculations and technical documentation to support our glider design decisions.

## My Contributions

As the team lead, I helped organize our schedule, divide responsibilities, and keep the project on track. I also worked alongside my three classmates on the design, purchasing materials, building the glider, conducting flight tests, calculating its performance, and writing the final report. Alongside coordinating the team, I contributed directly to the glider’s design and construction.

We used a work breakdown structure and Gantt chart to plan out the different stages of the project and to stay organized and meet our deadlines.

## Design Requirements

The glider was designed around several constraints including the following:

- Fuselage length and wingspan between 0.5 and 1.5 meters.
- Maximum empty weight below 15 N.
- No propulsion system.
- Pass beneath a 5 m wide, 3 m high arch positioned 5 m downstream from launch.
- Launch by hand from a stationary position, with a release height no greater than 2.5 m.
- Total project cost below $50.

## Glider Design and Construction

Our team decided to build the glider ourselves instead of buying a prebuilt one so we could have the freedom to make changes to its shape and design as the project progressed.

We chose balsa wood as our main building material because it is lightweight and easy to cut and work with. The glider consisted of the following parts: main wing, fuselage, horizontal stabilizer, and vertical stabilizer.

We also had to carry a payload of four quarters, so we paid attention to how the weight was distributed and where the center of gravity was located to help the glider maintain stable flight.

## Flight Testing and Results

On November 28, 2023, our team conducted two flight tests in the aerospace competition by hand-launching the glider. For both of the flights, the glider carried the required payload of four quarters and was released from a height of 70 inches. We also estimated the wind speed to be around 5 mph during testing. 

The test results from each trial are shown in the table below:

| Measurement | Trial 1 | Trial 2 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Horizontal range | 26 ft | 30 ft |
| Flight time | 2.5 s | 1.5 s |
| Payload | 4 quarters | 4 quarters |

Our longest recorded flight traveled approximately 30 feet, or about 9.14 meters. We noticed a slight difference in performance between the two flights; however, with only two tests, it was difficult to tell exactly what caused it. Factors like how the glider was launched, wind conditions, and the glider's configuration could all have affected the results.

## Performance Estimates and Limitations

Based on our flight test results from the competition, we calculated a glide angle of about 11°, an estimated lift-to-drag ratio of 5.14, and a wing loading of 15.9 N/m² with the payload included.

These numbers gave us a general idea of the glider's performance, but they were still preliminary estimates. For example, dividing the distance traveled during our second flight by its flight time gave us an average horizontal ground speed of approximately 6.1 m/s. This represents average horizontal ground speed; it does not directly measure airspeed, which also depends on the wind.

Our lift-to-drag ratio was also estimated using the glide angle, which basically assumes the glider was flying at a steady glide. Unfortunately, since our tests were short and hand-launched, we couldn't ever confirm that it maintained steady flight throughout the entire trajectory.

## Project Management and Budget

As the team lead, I helped keep everyone organized throughout the design, construction, testing, and technical documentation of the glider. We used a work breakdown structure (WBS) and Gantt chart to divide up all responsibilities, monitor our progress, and make sure that we all stayed on schedule.

We were given a maximum budget of $50; however, our final documented cost was only $23.74. This left us $26.26 under budget. Choosing to build the glider out of balsa wood helped us keep costs down while simultaneously giving us the flexibility to make changes to the design as we worked.

## Engineering Takeaways

This project gave me my first valuable hands-on aerospace engineering experience with designing and building an aircraft, choosing materials, conducting flight tests, and managing a project from start to finish.

It also helped me helped me better understand how a glider's shape, center of gravity, and overall design affect the way it flies. Being able to see these concepts in action during testing made them easier to understand beyond just classroom calculations.

Working with my teammates also taught me how to divide up technical responsibilities, stay within project constraints, and work together to turn an engineering design idea into something we could actually build and test.
